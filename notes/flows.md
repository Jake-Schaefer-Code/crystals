**Short Answer:** Yes. All those terms—“Hamiltonian flow,” “geodesic flow,” “gradient flow,” “mean curvature flow,” and “Ricci flow”—are indeed examples of a **flow** in the same sense we have been discussing (i.e., a one-parameter family of transformations satisfying the flow property). However, each of these phrases arises in a *specific* geometric or analytic context and is tied to a particular differential equation or geometric evolution.


Below is a bit more detail on each:

---

## 1. Hamiltonian Flow

- **Context:** Classical mechanics / Symplectic geometry.  
- **Basic idea:**  
  A Hamiltonian system on a symplectic manifold \((M, \omega)\) is given by a Hamiltonian function \(H: M \to \mathbb{R}\). The corresponding **Hamiltonian vector field** \(X_H\) is defined so that \(\omega(X_H, \cdot) = dH(\cdot)\).  
- **Flow definition:**  
  Solving the ODE \(\dot{z}(t) = X_H\bigl(z(t)\bigr)\) defines a *flow* \(\phi_H^t\) on the phase space \(M\). Concretely:
  \[
    \phi_H^0 = \mathrm{Id}, 
    \quad
    \phi_H^{t+s} = \phi_H^t \circ \phi_H^s,
  \]
  and \(\phi_H^t\) preserves the symplectic structure.  
- **Why it’s a “flow”:**  
  Just as with any ODE, you get a (local or global) one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms. The only difference from a basic ODE in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is that it happens on a **symplectic manifold** and preserves that structure.

---

## 2. Geodesic Flow

- **Context:** Differential geometry.  
- **Basic idea:**  
  On a Riemannian manifold \((M, g)\), a *geodesic* is a curve that locally minimizes distance (or equivalently has zero acceleration). A “geodesic flow” typically lives on the **tangent bundle** \(TM\).  
- **Flow definition:**  
  Each geodesic \(\gamma(t)\) in \(M\) has an initial condition \(\bigl(\gamma(0), \dot{\gamma}(0)\bigr)\) in \(TM\). One then defines \(\phi^t\bigl(x, v\bigr)\) to be “the point \(\bigl(\gamma(t), \dot{\gamma}(t)\bigr)\) obtained by following the geodesic with that initial data.”  
- **Why it’s a “flow”:**  
  The geodesic flow satisfies the same group property:
  \[
    \phi^0 = \mathrm{Id},
    \quad
    \phi^{t+s} = \phi^t \circ \phi^s.
  \]
  You flow along geodesics for time \(s\), then for time \(t\). It is precisely a continuous-time dynamical system, just on the tangent bundle of a manifold.

---

## 3. Gradient Flow

- **Context:** PDEs, calculus of variations, and functional analysis.  
- **Basic idea:**  
  If you have a function \(\Phi: X \to \mathbb{R}\) defined on a Riemannian manifold or a Banach space \(X\), the **gradient flow** of \(\Phi\) is the flow generated by the negative gradient vector field \(\nabla \Phi\).  
- **Flow definition:**  
  In finite dimensions, it’s the ODE
  \[
    \frac{d}{dt}x(t) \;=\; -\nabla \Phi\bigl(x(t)\bigr).
  \]
  In infinite dimensions (like a functional \(\Phi\) on a Sobolev space), you get a PDE that still defines a (semi)flow \(\phi_t\).  
- **Why it’s a “flow”:**  
  You get a **one-parameter semigroup** \(\phi_t\) (often forward in time only) such that
  \[
    \phi_0 = \mathrm{Id},
    \quad
    \phi_{t+s} = \phi_t \circ \phi_s,
  \]
  though it may not be invertible backward in time if \(\Phi\) is dissipative.

---

## 4. Mean Curvature Flow

- **Context:** Geometric evolution equations.  
- **Basic idea:**  
  Take a hypersurface \(\Sigma_0 \subset \mathbb{R}^{n+1}\). Let it evolve so that each point on the surface moves **normal to the surface** with velocity equal to its **mean curvature** \(H\).  
- **Flow definition:**  
  One might write 
  \[
    \frac{\partial}{\partial t} F(p,t) 
      \;=\; - H\bigl(F(p,t)\bigr)\,\mathbf{n}(p,t),
  \]
  with \(F(p,0)=\Sigma_0\), where \(\mathbf{n}\) is the unit normal. This PDE defines a **family** \(\Sigma_t = F(\Sigma_0,t)\).  
- **Why it’s a “flow”:**  
  For each \(t\), you get a new embedded hypersurface \(\Sigma_t\). The map \(\Sigma_0 \mapsto \Sigma_t\) is (at least for short times) a continuous evolution in time, satisfying \(\Sigma_{t+s} = \Sigma_t \circ \Sigma_s\) in an appropriate sense. It’s often called a **geometric flow**.

---

## 5. Ricci Flow

- **Context:** Riemannian geometry.  
- **Basic idea:**  
  Invented by Richard Hamilton (and famously used by Grigori Perelman to prove the Poincaré conjecture), Ricci flow deforms a Riemannian metric \(g(t)\) on a manifold according to its Ricci curvature \(\mathrm{Ric}(g)\):
  \[
    \frac{\partial}{\partial t}g(t) 
      \;=\; -2\,\mathrm{Ric}\bigl(g(t)\bigr).
  \]
- **Flow definition:**  
  One starts with an initial metric \(g(0)\). Then \(g(t)\) solves this PDE in \(\text{Sym}^2(T^*M)\). Each time \(t\) yields a new metric, hence a new geometry on the same manifold.  
- **Why it’s a “flow”:**  
  We get a one-parameter family of metrics \(\{g(t)\}\), each smoothly depending on \(t\). The PDE ensures that if you “flow” for time \(t\) and then for time \(s\), you are effectively “flowing” for time \(t+s\). It matches the conceptual definition of a flow (though it’s typically a *semiflow* if backward-in-time solutions do not exist for certain singularities).

---

## 6. Are They the Same as the General “Flow” Definition?

**Yes**—all of these are “flows” in the same foundational sense:

- Each is parameterized by \(\mathbb{R}\) (often \(\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}\) if it’s not invertible backward in time).
- Each has an evolution law (often a PDE or ODE) that ensures the **semigroup property** (or group property if the evolution is invertible backward in time).
- Each can be regarded as a **one-parameter family of transformations** on some set (manifold, metric space, function space, etc.)—exactly the idea that \(\phi^{t+s} = \phi^t \circ \phi^s\).

They just come from **specific** PDEs or vector fields in geometry/physics and therefore acquire special names and additional structure (symplectic structure, curvature terms, gradient structure, etc.).

---

## Final Summary

- **All** “XYZ flow” phrases in mathematics mean that we have a **time-dependent evolution** defined by a PDE or ODE that (locally, at least) forms a one-parameter (semi)group of transformations.  
- The word “flow” is *exactly* in the sense we described: “\(\phi(x,t)\)” satisfies \(\phi(\phi(x,s),t) = \phi(x,s+t)\).  
- Each specialized name (Hamiltonian, geodesic, gradient, mean curvature, Ricci) reflects the particular **geometric or analytic** context (symplectic geometry, Riemannian geometry, functionals, hypersurface evolution, metric evolution, etc.).
