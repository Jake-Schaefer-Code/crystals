# Algebraic Geometry for the 1D Fermion Problem

Oct 7, 2026 · @Jake

A route from invariant theory to a working, determinant-free VMC ansatz — targeted at a JAX stack with Gröbner and finite-group-representation machinery.

## The 1D factorization theorem

In one dimension every antisymmetric function is the Vandermonde determinant times a symmetric function — exactly, with nothing left over and no approximation.

$$
\psi(x_1,\dots,x_N) \;=\; \Delta(x)\,S(x), \qquad \Delta(x) = \prod_{1 \le i < j \le N} (x_j - x_i)
$$

For polynomials this is a divisibility statement. An antisymmetric polynomial vanishes on every hyperplane $x_i = x_j$, i.e., the ideal $\langle x_j - x_i\rangle$, so each linear form $x_j − x_i$ divides it, and since those forms are *coprime* their product does. The quotient is symmetric. For smooth non-polynomial wavefunctions the same holds with S continuous and symmetric.

Now apply the *fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials*: the invariant ring is **free**, generated by the elementary symmetric polynomials with no relations among them.

$$
\mathbb{C}[x_1,\dots,x_N]^{S_N} \;=\; \mathbb{C}[e_1,\dots,e_N].
$$

Put the two together and we have a complete, unconstrained parametrization of the antisymmetric sector:

$$
\psi(x) \;=\; \Delta(x)\cdot f\big(e_1(x),\dots,e_N(x)\big)
$$

with $f$ an **arbitrary** function of $N$ variables. There is no permutation symmetry to impose on $f$ (No determinant) — it is automatic by definition, because the $e_k$ are already invariant. That is the whole ansatz, and it is **exact**.

### Why this collapses in 3D

The obstruction is a statement about modules, not about intuition. Over the invariant ring, the sign-isotypic component of the polynomial ring is free of rank one in 1D, generated by the Vandermonde:
$$
\begin{bmatrix}
  1 & x_0 & x_0^2 & \cdots &x_0^n \\
  1 & x_1 & x_1^2 & \cdots &x_1^n \\
  \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
  1 & x_m & x_m^2 & \cdots &x_m^n \\
\end{bmatrix}
$$


For the diagonal action of $S_N$ on $N$ copies of $R^3$ it is not free of rank one — it needs many generators, and there is no single universal antisymmetric factor to pull out.

Concretely: $x_j − x_i$ is a scalar that carries a sign. In 3D the difference $r_j − r_i$ is a vector, and its norm is symmetric, so neither one gives you a Vandermonde. That single fact is why 1D is solved and 3D is not.

## The chamber: no sign problem at all

Restrict to the ordered region and the sign disappears from the computation entirely. This is the practical payoff of the factorization, and it is why the 1D implementation can be exact rather than fixed-node-approximate.

$S_N$ acts on $\mathbb R^N$ by reflections in the hyperplanes $x_i = x_j$. A *fundamental domain* is the open *Weyl chamber*

$$
\mathcal{C} \;=\; \{\, x \in \mathbb{R}^N \;:\; x_1 < x_2 < \cdots < x_N \,\},
$$

and every antisymmetric function is determined by its restriction to it, with Dirichlet conditions on the walls. Three consequences that matter for code:

1. **The exact node is known.** It is the union of the walls, the zero set of the Vandermonde, and nothing else. Fixed-node DMC in 1D is therefore not an approximation — it is exact.
2. **The wavefunction has one sign.** Inside the chamber, the Vandermonde is strictly positive and $f$ is a scalar function, so psi does not change sign. One works with log psi throughout and never track a determinant sign or a permutation parity.
3. **Sampling is trivial to constrain.** Sort the walker after every proposal, or reject any move that crosses a wall. Both are correct; sorting is cheaper and keeps detailed balance because the sorted configuration is the unique chamber representative of its orbit.

The group-theoretic bookkeeping reduces to two facts your rep-theory layer already has: the adjacent transpositions generate S\_N, and the sign of a permutation is the parity of its inversion count, which for a sorting network is just the number of swaps performed.

This is also the precise sense in which 1D is the easy case. The coincidence set has codimension 1, so it disconnects configuration space into chambers you can cut along. In 3D it has codimension 3, the complement stays connected, no ordering exists, and none of this is available.

## The ansatz in code

Work in the log domain inside the chamber, where everything is positive:

$$
\log \psi(x) \;=\; \sum_{i<j} \log\,(x_j - x_i) \;+\; f_\theta\big(\sigma(x)\big)
$$

with $\sigma(x)$ a vector of symmetric features and $f_\theta$ an ordinary MLP. The first term is $O(N^2)$ (pairwise in $N$ interactions) and needs no determinant; the second is unconstrained because its input is already invariant. Both are trivially jittable and differentiate cleanly for the local energy.

### Choosing the symmetric features

This is the one real engineering decision, and the exact-theory answer is the wrong one numerically.

| Feature set | Exactness | Conditioning | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Elementary symmetric $e_1\ldots e_N$ | Exact and free-generating | Dynamic range grows like $N!$; overflows by $N\approx 15$ | Correct on paper, unusable as network input |
| Power sums $p_k$, $k = 1\ldots N$ | Exact generating set in char $0$ (Newton's identities) | Still grows, but tameable by scaling as $p_k$ over $N$, to the power $1/k$ | Workable to moderate $N$ |
| DeepSets, sum over $i$ of $\phi(x_i)$ | Universal for continuous symmetric functions | Well conditioned by construction | Best default in practice |

The honest framing: the invariant-theory statement tells you the ansatz class is **complete**, and DeepSets is a well-conditioned way to realize that same class approximately. Start with DeepSets plus a handful of scaled power sums concatenated, and keep the exact $e_k$ path available only as a small-$N$ correctness check against your Gröbner layer.

### Numerical notes

- Compute $e_k$, when you want them, as the coefficients of the product over $i$ of $(t − x_i)$ by the $O(N^2)$ convolution recurrence. Never as an explicit sum over subsets.
- Compute the Vandermonde term as a sum of logs of gaps, never as a product then a log. Gaps are strictly positive in the chamber, so no absolute value and no sign bookkeeping is needed.
- The log derivative needed for the drift has a closed form: the gradient of the Vandermonde term with respect to $x_i$ is a sum of reciprocal gaps. Hard-code it rather than leaning on autodiff through the log, which is less stable near coalescence.
- The local energy needs a Laplacian. For $N$ up to a few dozen, forward-over-reverse Hessian diagonal via ``jax.jvp`` over ``jax.grad`` is fine; above that use a Hutchinson estimator.

## What Gröbner bases actually buy you

Gröbner is offline tooling, not an inner-loop component. It proves things about small cases and generates structure you then hard-code; it never runs inside the Monte Carlo. Keep that boundary sharp or the project stalls on a Buchberger run that does not terminate.

Four tasks where it does real work:

**1. Symmetric-function rewriting, as a correctness oracle.** 
Given a candidate antisymmetric polynomial, divide by the Vandermonde and express the quotient in the elementary symmetric polynomials. The classical algorithm is a Gröbner computation: take the ideal generated by $(e_k(x) − E_k)$ for $k = 1\ldots N$ 
in the ring with both the $x$ and $E$ variables, use a LEX order eliminating the $x$, and reduce. This gives a machine-checkable version of the theorem in Section 1 for $N$ up to about $6$, which is exactly the regime where you want to be sure your JAX feature maps are right.

**2. The Plücker ideal as an explicit object.** For $N$ fermions in $M$ orbitals, the single-Slater-determinant states form the Grassmannian inside projective space, cut out by the Plücker quadrics. Computing that ideal for small $(N, M)$ gives you a quantitative correlation measure: take any CI vector, evaluate the Plücker polynomials on it, and the residual is the distance from being a single determinant. For $N = 2$, $M = 4$ the ideal is one quadric, the Klein quadric, which is exactly the $4$-by-$4$ Pfaffian — a one-line test you can write today.

**3. Nodal-variety saturation.** For a polynomial trial function, ask whether its node is *only* the coincidence set. Compute the saturation of the ideal generated by psi by the Vandermonde, taken to arbitrary power. If the result is the unit ideal, psi has no spurious nodes; otherwise the saturation ideal is precisely the extra nodal surface. This is a genuine Gröbner computation with direct physical meaning, and it is the one place where algebraic geometry says something about nodes that is both constructive and checkable.

**4. Molien series as a unit test.** The dimension of the space of degree-d antisymmetric polynomials in N variables has a closed-form generating function:

```latex
\sum_{d \ge 0} \dim \Lambda_d \, q^{\,d} \;=\; \frac{q^{\,N(N-1)/2}}{\prod_{k=1}^{N} \left(1 - q^{\,k}\right)}
```

The numerator is the degree of the Vandermonde; the denominator is the free invariant ring. Your rep-theory layer can derive the same number from the Molien series for the sign representation. Two independent routes to one integer makes an excellent first test that both layers of your library agree.

### The complexity wall

Buchberger is doubly exponential in the worst case. Plan for N at most 5 or 6 with generic input, and expect the Plücker ideal to be fine well beyond that only because its generators are known in closed form (the three-term relations for two-particle cases, the straightening law in general) — so use the known generators and treat Gröbner as verification, not discovery.

## What finite group representation theory buys you

The rep-theory layer earns its keep on spin, not on space. Spatial antisymmetry in 1D is already solved by the Vandermonde; what is left genuinely needs Young diagrams.

**Spin adaptation without spin coordinates.** For spin-1/2 fermions the total wavefunction is antisymmetric, but the spatial part is not — it carries a mixed-symmetry irrep of S\_N, conjugate to the spin irrep. For spin 1/2 only Young diagrams with at most two columns occur. If you build the spatial irrep projectors from Young symmetrizers, you get a spin-adapted spatial wavefunction of definite total S with the spin degrees of freedom integrated out entirely. That is a real reduction: no spin variables in the walker, no spin-flip moves, and an exact good quantum number.

This is the one place where your group-theory library does something your JAX layer cannot fake. Concretely you need: standard Young tableaux enumeration for a given shape, the Young orthogonal (or seminormal) representation matrices for adjacent transpositions, and the projector onto the chosen irrep component.

**Schur functions as exact benchmarks.** The bialternant formula is the Vandermonde factorization made explicit for free fermions:

```latex
s_\lambda(x_1,\dots,x_N) \;=\; \frac{\det\!\left(x_i^{\,\lambda_j + N - j}\right)}{\prod_{i<j}(x_j - x_i)}
```

The numerator is a Slater determinant of monomial orbitals; the denominator is the Vandermonde; the quotient is the symmetric function your network is supposed to learn. So for free fermions you know the exact target of f\_theta in closed form, indexed by a partition. Free-fermion occupation patterns map to Young diagrams map to Schur functions — and those Schur functions are simultaneously the characters of GL irreps and the Plücker coordinates from the previous section. Three descriptions of one object, all computable in your library.

**Chamber bookkeeping.** Minor but worth having in one place: adjacent transpositions as generators, the length function as inversion count, the sign character as its parity, and the chamber as the fundamental domain. Your sampler needs exactly this and nothing more.

## Benchmarks, in order

Start with Calogero–Sutherland, because its exact ground state lies **inside** your ansatz class. If the code cannot recover it to near machine precision, the bug is in the implementation, not the physics — which is exactly the property you want from a first test.

### 1. Calogero–Sutherland (do this first)

N particles in a harmonic trap with inverse-square interaction. The exact ground state is

```latex
\psi_0(x) \;=\; \prod_{i<j} |x_i - x_j|^{\,\lambda} \; \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\,\omega \sum_i x_i^2\right)
```

which is literally Vandermonde to a power times a symmetric function. Your network only has to learn a quadratic in the second power sum. The exact ground-state energy is known in closed form, so you get a hard number to hit. At lambda = 1 this reduces to free fermions, giving you a second check from the same code path.

### 2. Free fermions in a trap or on a ring

Exact Slater determinant of single-particle states, and via the bialternant formula an exact Schur-function target for f\_theta. Use this to validate the symmetric-feature layer specifically: you can compare the learned f against the known symmetric function pointwise, not just compare energies.

### 3. Interacting cases with exact answers

| System | Why it is useful | What is exact |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tonks–Girardeau limit | Hardcore bosons map to free fermions; tests the chamber logic | Full spectrum |
| Lieb–Liniger (fermionic cousin) | Bethe ansatz integrable; genuine interaction | Ground-state energy |
| 1D Hubbard | Lattice analogue; Lieb–Wu solution | Ground-state energy |

### 4. Something with no exact answer

Only after the three above pass. A 1D system with a potential you invent, where the point is now variance minimization and the zero-variance principle rather than agreement with a known number.

### Tests to write alongside

- [ ] Antisymmetry: evaluate psi off-chamber via the sorted representative and a parity factor, confirm sign flips under a transposition
- [ ] Molien check: dimension of degree-d antisymmetric polynomials, Gröbner route versus character-theory route, N up to 5
- [ ] Vandermonde gradient: closed-form reciprocal-gap formula versus autodiff, agreement to tolerance
- [ ] Plücker: a random single Slater determinant has vanishing Pfaffian residual; a random CI vector does not
- [ ] Energy variance goes to zero as the ansatz approaches the Calogero–Sutherland exact state

### Four failure modes, all the same bug

Getting this to converge took four fixes, and every one of them is the same underlying condition: **the local energy must stay integrable at particle coalescence.** They are worth knowing in advance because three of them look like optimization problems and are not.

1. **float32 is not enough.** Second derivatives of log psi in single precision leave about 1e-3 of noise in the local energy, which completely hides the zero-variance signal. Enable x64 before importing jax.numpy.
2. **The Vandermonde power is fixed by physics, not learned — but not for the reason you might expect.** For a pair interaction g over r squared, the cusp condition alpha(alpha − 1) = g pins the short-range behaviour, and away from it the local energy keeps a residual (g − alpha(alpha − 1)) / r squared, positive below the cusp. The variational principle survives this intact: with equilibrated walkers the true mean of E\_L is minimized exactly at the cusp and equals E\_0 there. What fails is the *estimator*. The entire penalty for missing the cusp lives in a rare positive tail, and the variance of E\_L is infinite for alpha below 3/2, so the gradient needs clipping — and clipping is precisely what deletes the penalty. Measured at n = 3, lambda = 2, E\_0 = 7.5: the true mean is minimized at alpha = 2.00, the 5-MAD-clipped mean at alpha = 1.90 (value 7.4972), the median at alpha = 1.60 (value 7.3728). The last two are below E\_0, so a gradient built on either is pulled off the cusp, correctly optimizing the wrong objective. Pin alpha analytically; with it pinned the tail disappears and clipping stops mattering.
3. **Nothing else may be log-singular at coalescence either.** The network rediscovers the cusp violation through any door left open: log(gap) as a Jastrow input, log(gap) or min(gap) in the feature map, or the sign-times-k-th-root power-sum scaling whose derivative diverges as a power sum crosses zero (and the centre of mass crosses zero constantly). All three produced variance spikes of 10^3 to 10^4 here. Every feature must be smooth and bounded near coalescence; all short-range behaviour belongs to alpha alone.
4. **The envelope is not optional.** alpha times log V is not normalizable on its own. Without a confining factor the walkers diffuse outward and the trap term in the potential drives the energy up without bound. The network learns a correction to the envelope, never the decay itself.

The general lesson generalizes past 1D: in this ansatz family the cusp structure is a hard constraint on the function class, and anything in the architecture that can represent a logarithm of an interparticle distance is a place where the optimizer can cheat.

## Limits, stated plainly

**None of the 1D machinery survives the move to 3D.** This is worth internalizing before investing heavily. The factorization theorem rests on x\_j − x\_i being a scalar that carries a sign; in 3D the difference is a vector and its norm is symmetric. The module-theoretic version: the sign-isotypic component is free of rank one over the invariants in 1D and is not in 3D. There is no Vandermonde to pull out, no total order, no chamber, and no Dirichlet reformulation. What you build here is a complete solution to a genuinely easier problem.

**What does carry over.** Three things, in decreasing order of confidence:

- The equivariance principle. Any S\_N-equivariant map applied before the antisymmetrizing step preserves antisymmetry. This is dimension-independent and is what backflow and the FermiNet family exploit.
- The cusp-as-hard-constraint lesson from Section 7. Short-range behaviour is determined by the potential, not learnable, and architectures that can represent a logarithm of an interparticle distance give the optimizer a way to cheat. That is a 3D problem too, and a more severe one.
- Plücker residuals as a correlation diagnostic. Dimension-independent, cheap, and genuinely informative about how far a state is from a single determinant.

**Constructive versus descriptive.** Be honest about which column each idea sits in:

| Idea | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Vandermonde × symmetric in 1D | Constructive and exact — it is the ansatz |
| Schur / bialternant benchmarks | Constructive — exact pointwise targets |
| Young symmetrizers for spin adaptation | Constructive — removes spin variables outright |
| Plücker ideal as correlation measure | Constructive but diagnostic, not generative |
| Molien series checks | Constructive for testing only |
| Nodal surfaces as real algebraic varieties | Descriptive — no QMC win has come from it yet |

**The Gröbner wall.** Buchberger is doubly exponential in the worst case. Budget N at most 5 or 6 for generic input. The Plücker ideal is an exception only because its generators are known in closed form, so use the known generators and treat Gröbner as verification rather than discovery. Nothing symbolic belongs in the Monte Carlo inner loop.

**The deeper caveat on nodes.** The topology of configuration space tells you that a sign structure must exist and, in 3D, that it can only be plus or minus one. It is deliberately blind to geometry. Every trick that has actually bought something — exact nodes for triplet helium, the Vandermonde factorization, flux attachment in the lowest Landau level — works in the geometry, and uses the topology only to know what it is allowed to get away with. Algebraic geometry describes the space of antisymmetric objects well. It does not yet tell you which one nature picked.
